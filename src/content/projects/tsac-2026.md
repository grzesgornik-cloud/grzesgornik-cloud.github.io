## Why it needed redesigning

For 2026 the electrical team were moving to a new battery management system (BMS), which meant a new battery too. The old accumulator container was made of steel. It was strong, but heavy, and it had been designed around different cells and electronics, so we started again.

The accumulator container (the TSAC, short for tractive system accumulator container) gets a lot of attention at scrutineering. It has to hold the cells in place through the crash load cases in the rules, and we have to show that it does in the Structural Equivalency Spreadsheet (SES) before the car is allowed to run.

## My part

The electrical team looked after the cells, the BMS and the wiring. I worked on the mechanical side with them: the walls, the internal dividers, the lid and the mounts, and fitting the cooling fans and connectors in.

I modelled it in Onshape and went back and forth on the layout with the electrical team until everything fit inside the space we had. Moving from steel to aluminium was where most of the weight came from, but it also meant I had to be careful with the thickness of every wall and divider.

![Onshape render of the closed container, with cooling fans on one end and mounting feet along the base](/projects/tsac-2026/cad-render.png)

## Checking it in Ansys

I ran static structural analyses in Ansys against the SES load cases and used the results to decide where I could take material out and where it needed to stay. The final design has a minimum safety factor of 1.95.

![Ansys safety factor contour plot of the container, minimum 1.95](/projects/tsac-2026/fea-safety-factor.png)

## How it turned out

The new container is 8 kg lighter than the steel one it replaced, and it passed SES. It was built and went to competition on the 2026 car.
